FAA owner obligations

What the FAA expects of a registered owner, and how we meet it

William H. Gilliam · November Aircraft Registration

In brief

The FAA registers aircraft to owners, not to operators. In 2013 it said plainly what that means: every registered owner carries the same obligations, and no owner can hand them to someone else by contract. The owner does not have to fly the aircraft, but it has to know who does and be able to answer for it.

That is why we form one United States company for each aircraft. It is more work than adding an aircraft to a shared arrangement. It also ties the aircraft directly to the people concerned with it: the owner who uses and pays for it, and the US citizen who answers to the FAA for it. Nobody else stands in between, and nothing is shared with any other owner.

What the FAA said

The source is the FAA's Notice of Policy Clarification, 78 Fed. Reg. 36412 (June 18, 2013). It was written about owner trusts, the most common route to a US registration for a non-US owner. Its statements about owners are general.

  • All owners are treated alike. Individuals, partnerships, corporations and associations have the same obligation to comply, and the registered owner “is the owner for all purposes under the regulations” (page 36414).
  • A contract does not move the obligation. “No owner of an aircraft on the U.S. registry can avoid a regulatory obligation imposed on it by the FAA simply by entering into a private contract with another party” (page 36414).
  • The owner must know the operation. The FAA expects an owner normally to be able to say, within two business days, who operates or manages the aircraft, where that person is, where the records are kept and where the aircraft is based. Within five business days it expects details of specific flights, maintenance records and airworthiness status. These are guidelines, not fixed deadlines (pages 36414 to 36415).
  • Aircraft kept abroad get the closest attention. Where an aircraft is used in general aviation outside the United States and the FAA cannot readily identify the operator, it looks to the registered owner (page 36415).
  • Control is read from the documents. The FAA reviews who can direct or remove the US citizen who holds title, and it asks for every side agreement that gives the non-US party possession and use of the aircraft (pages 36415 to 36417).

Why one company for one aircraft

Forming a separate company for every aircraft means separate formation papers, a separate statement to the FAA, and separate records. A shared trustee or a company holding several aircraft would be less work.

The separate company is worth the work because of what the FAA expects. When the FAA asks about an aircraft, there is one company that owns it, one set of records, and one US citizen responsible for the answer. The aircraft is tied to the people actually concerned with it and to no one else. Another owner's difficulty cannot reach your registration, because no other owner is in your company.

How each of our companies meets these expectations

  • A US company holds title. Each company is organized in the United States for one aircraft and holds nothing else.
  • A US citizen holds the vote. William H. Gilliam, a US citizen, holds the voting interest and exercises it independently. The non-US owner holds a non-voting interest that carries the whole economic benefit and cost of the aircraft.
  • Succession is written in. Each company's governing documents name a US-citizen successor to the voting interest.
  • The FAA sees the structure. The statement filed with the FAA for each company identifies its members, their citizenship and who manages it. The non-US owner's interest is disclosed in that statement.
  • We know the operation. For each aircraft we keep current the name and location of the operator, the place the aircraft is based, and where its maintenance records are held, so that an FAA request can be answered on the timelines above.
  • FAA mail reaches the owner. We open every FAA letter for the aircraft and send the owner a copy, including airworthiness and registration notices.
  • The use arrangement is consistent with control. The agreement under which the owner uses the aircraft does not give the owner power to direct or remove the holder of the voting interest.

What this page is not

This page describes what the FAA has published and how we organize our own companies. It is general information, not legal or tax advice. The FAA's 2013 notice addresses owner trusts; it does not approve or disapprove any particular company. Whether US registration suits your aircraft depends on your circumstances, and you should take advice from your own counsel in your own country.
