One LLC for your aircraft

One on One

William H. Gilliam · November Aircraft Registration

Think your trust is blind? Blink again.

Stop and think for a moment. How blind is a registration placed with a trustee who acts for many others? The trustee's name sits on the registry beside every aircraft it holds. That is not a blind. It is a sign, a signpost, a signal of what the aircraft is: one of many held in trust for someone else. One on one is the opposite. The name on your registration belongs to a company that owns your aircraft and nothing else.

Think of it as a long-term decision. The name on the registration stays with the aircraft for as long as you operate it.

One aircraft, one company

An owner who is neither a citizen nor a resident of the United States generally cannot put an aircraft on the US registry in his own name. There are a small number of lawful routes to an N-number for such an owner. The one I have used since the late 1980s is the simplest to describe: one aircraft, one company.

Each aircraft I register is owned by its own domestic limited liability company, formed for that aircraft and for nothing else. The company owns one airplane. It has no other business and no other assets, and it is independent of every other company I manage. Nothing is commingled.

Inside the company, ownership is divided into two kinds of shares. The non-US owner holds the non-voting shares, and the articles give that owner all of the economic use, benefits and costs of the aircraft. I hold the voting shares, and with them control of the company. The owner's use agreement gives the owner the aircraft to fly, but no power to direct or remove me. None of this is hidden: the owner's interest is disclosed in the statement filed with the FAA.

One company per aircraft looks like more work than putting many aircraft under one roof, and it is. What it buys is a direct tie between each aircraft and the people concerned with it. Your aircraft does not share an owner of record with a stranger's aircraft. The company's records are about one airplane and one owner, and a question about someone else's airplane is not a question about yours.

Control carries duties, and I treat them as mine. For every company I keep current who operates the aircraft, where it is based and where its maintenance records are kept. I open every letter the FAA sends and send the owner a copy.

The structure was also built to outlast me. Each company has a succession in place naming a US citizen, so the registration does not depend on any one person's continued presence.

That is what one on one means: one aircraft, one company, one owner, and one person answerable for it.
