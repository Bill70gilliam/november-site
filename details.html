Still here: a forthright word on registering your aircraft in the United States

William H. Gilliam · November Aircraft Registration

One aircraft, one companyYour aircraft is the only asset of a US company formed for it.
Not a pooled structureUnlike a trust company, where one trustee holds title for many owners and many kinds of aircraft, your company holds your aircraft only. It is built so that no other owner's problem can disrupt it.
Nothing pooledNo aircraft, money or records shared with any other owner.
Succession in placeA US-citizen successor is already provided for.

I have been registering aircraft with the FAA for owners who are not US citizens since the late 1980s. I had planned to wind this work down quietly. I have changed my mind, and I want to tell you why.

In January 2026 the FAA declared every registration held through one well-known trustee invalid. By most accounts about 800 aircraft were grounded the same day. The FAA said the company did not meet the US citizenship requirement for a trustee. The company disputed that, and its trusts were later taken over by a US trust firm. I take no position on who was right. What matters to an owner is simpler. People who had paid their fees and done nothing wrong woke up to an aircraft they could not legally fly, and to a warning from aviation authorities that their insurance might be affected as well.

The rule behind it is not complicated. The US register is a register of owners, and the owner must be a US citizen or a resident alien. Congress wrote that citizenship rule in 1926 chiefly with national defense in mind: aircraft on the US register were to be owned by citizens the country could call on, with compensation, in an emergency or a war. The structure I use keeps faith with that purpose. Legal title and the vote stay with a US citizen. A non-US owner usually gets there through an owner trust. A US-citizen trustee holds legal title and the aircraft is registered in the trustee's name, while the owner keeps the benefit and the use of it. There are other routes, such as a voting trust for some companies, or registering an aircraft that is based and primarily used in the United States. The trust is the common one. The FAA has accepted it for decades and set out what it expects in 2013.

So everything rests on who holds the title and the vote. That is the part most websites spend the least time on.

I have looked at who offers this work today, and it falls into roughly four kinds. First are the banks and large trust companies. They hold hundreds or thousands of aircraft, mostly business jets and financed transactions. They are solid, and they are priced and staffed for that market. You will be an account number. Second are the specialist trust companies that serve light aircraft in volume. Many are perfectly good. The January grounding came out of this part of the market. Third are the filing websites that look official. They are private companies, and the better ones say in the small print that they are not the FAA. They prepare forms. The FAA's own registration fee is five dollars. There is nothing wrong with paying for help, but know what you are paying for. Fourth are the aviation law firms, which are excellent for a complicated transaction and expensive for a simple one.

Whoever you use, ask these questions. Ask me too.

  • Who exactly will hold title to my aircraft, and where was that person or company formed?
  • Are the people who own and control it US citizens, and will they say so in writing?
  • Will I receive a copy of every document filed with the FAA about my aircraft?
  • When the FAA writes about my aircraft, who opens the letter, and how soon do I hear about it?
  • What happens to my registration if you sell the business, retire or die?

That last question is one I owe you an answer to more than most, having done this for close to forty years. Here it is. Each company's governing documents provide for a successor, who is also a US citizen, to take over the voting interest if I can no longer act. That was written in from the start, with my own end in mind, so that your registration does not depend on my being here.

I should also say what a US registration is not. It is not a tax plan. An N-number does not by itself change what you owe in your own country, and it does not excuse you from the rules of the country where the aircraft is kept and flown. Anyone who implies otherwise is selling something. Take your own tax and legal advice. I am not a substitute for it.

How I do it is simple to describe. Each aircraft is owned by its own US company, formed for that aircraft and holding nothing else. One company, one aircraft. I am a US citizen and I hold the voting interest, which I exercise independently. You hold the entire economic interest: the use, the benefit and the cost of the aircraft are yours alone. Nothing of yours is pooled with any other owner's aircraft, money or records. Each company stands on its own, always, and no other owner's difficulty can reach your registration.

Beyond that, what I offer is one person. I am the same person who has been doing this since the late 1980s. When you write, I am the one who answers. I look at eligibility and the ownership arrangement first, and I will tell you plainly if a US registration is the wrong answer for you. If it is the right one, I prepare the documents, file them, and follow them through the registry until the certificate is issued. I am not a bank, and I will not pretend to be one.

I intended to fade. Then I watched hundreds of owners grounded over a question that should have been asked on the first day. Getting that question right on the first day is what this work is. So I am staying.
